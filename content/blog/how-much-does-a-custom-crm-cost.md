"How much will it cost?" is the first question almost every business owner asks, and the most common answer is the least helpful one: *"It depends."*

It does depend, but not in a mysterious way. Once you understand the handful of things that drive the price, you can estimate your own project, compare quotes fairly, and avoid paying for features you don't need yet. That's what this guide is for.

## First, decide if you need a *custom* CRM at all

A CRM (customer relationship management system) is simply one place to keep track of your leads, clients, deals and follow-ups. You have two ways to get one:

| | Off-the-shelf CRM (HubSpot, Zoho, Pipedrive, etc.) | Custom CRM (built for you) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **How you pay** | Monthly subscription, usually per user | One-time build, plus small running costs |
| **Time to start** | Same day | Usually several weeks |
| **Fits your process?** | You adapt your process to the tool | The tool adapts to your process |
| **Cost as you grow** | Rises with every user and premium feature | Mostly flat, since you own it |
| **Best for** | Standard sales pipelines, small teams, trying things out | Unique workflows, many users, or connecting tightly to your other systems |

**Be honest with yourself here.** If a ready-made CRM covers 80% of what you need, start there. A custom build pays off when you keep fighting the tool, pay for many seats, or need something no subscription does well, such as a pipeline that mirrors exactly how your business works or a system that connects to your booking, inventory or billing tools.

## Why developers talk in hours, not prices

Custom software is priced by the **time** it takes to design, build and test it. That's why the most reliable way to think about cost is:

> **Estimated hours × the developer's hourly rate = project cost**

Rates vary a lot by country, experience and whether you hire a freelancer or an agency, so the *hours* are the part you can actually reason about. If two developers quote very different prices, ask each one for their hour estimate. It tells you whether they're pricing the same scope.

## The 7 things that drive your CRM's hours

1. **Number of screens.** Every unique page (contact list, contact details, pipeline board, reports, settings) needs to be designed, built and tested. More screens means more hours.
2. **User roles and permissions.** "Everyone sees everything" is simple. "Sales reps see only their own clients, managers see the team, admins manage users" takes more work, and matters for security.
3. **Your workflow.** A basic *Lead → Proposal → Won* pipeline is quick. Custom stages, required fields, approvals and automatic reminders add time.
4. **Integrations.** Connecting to email, your website forms, accounting software, payments or messaging apps is often where the surprises hide. Each integration is a small project of its own.
5. **Reports and dashboards.** A few key numbers are easy. Custom charts, filters and exports take longer.
6. **Moving your existing data.** Importing years of messy spreadsheets needs cleaning and checking. Budget time for it; it's rarely "just upload the file."
7. **Design.** A clean, easy-to-use interface costs more upfront but pays for itself when your team actually *wants* to use the system.

## Typical size ranges (in hours)

These are realistic ranges for a well-built CRM, including design, development, testing and launch. Your exact number depends on the details above.

| CRM size | What's usually included | Typical effort |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Starter** | Contacts, companies, a simple deal pipeline, notes, one or two user types | about 120–200 hours |
| **Growing team** | Everything above, plus user roles, a dashboard, reminders and follow-up tasks, data import | about 200–350 hours |
| **Advanced** | Custom workflows and approvals, several integrations, client portal, detailed reports | 350+ hours |

## Don't forget the running costs

The build is usually the biggest cost, but a few smaller ones continue after launch:

- **Hosting and database.** Many modern platforms have free or low-cost starting tiers, and costs grow with usage.
- **Domain and email sending** (for notifications and reminders).
- **Maintenance.** Security updates, small fixes and improvements. Many businesses keep a small monthly support arrangement or book changes as needed.
- **Third-party services** you connect, such as an SMS or payment provider.

Ask any developer to list these clearly before you start. There should be no surprises after launch.

## 6 ways to keep the cost down (without cutting corners)

1. **Start with a Minimum Viable Product (MVP).** Build the core, meaning contacts, the pipeline and follow-ups, and launch. Add reports and automations in phase two once your team has used it.
2. **Write down your process first.** A one-page description of how a lead becomes a client saves hours of back-and-forth. (See [What to prepare before hiring a developer](/blog/what-to-prepare-before-hiring-a-developer/).)
3. **Reuse what works.** Keep your accounting or email tool and *connect* to it, rather than rebuilding it inside your CRM.
4. **Clean your data before migration.** Removing duplicates and dead leads yourself is cheaper than paying someone to do it.
5. **Limit custom reports at first.** Decide on the 5 numbers you check every week and start with those.
6. **Choose a fixed-scope quote.** Agreeing on what's included up front protects both sides from budget creep.

## Questions to ask before you sign

- What exactly is included, screen by screen?
- How many rounds of design changes are included?
- What happens after launch: is there a bug-fix period, and what does support cost?
- Who owns the code and the data?
- What are the monthly running costs, and who pays them?
- Can we build it in phases?

A good developer will answer all of these clearly and in writing.

## The bottom line

A custom CRM isn't priced by magic. It's the sum of screens, roles, workflow, integrations, reports, data migration and design. Start by asking whether an off-the-shelf tool is enough. If it isn't, define your core process, build an MVP first, and grow it with your business.
